## Introduction

Audiovisual works do not simply record the world. They organise images and sounds through choices of framing, duration, editing, point of view, rhythm and sound. These choices influence what viewers notice, how they connect images and how they interpret a scene.

*Exploring Audiovisual Grammar: Tools and Methods for Video Analysis* introduces six fundamental operations of audiovisual language. Each module is built around a short episode in which the same everyday situation is presented in five different ways. Comparing these variations makes it possible to isolate particular formal choices and observe how they transform the viewer’s experience.

The course combines observation, analysis, collaborative annotation and practical experimentation. Its purpose is not to establish a single correct interpretation, but to provide learners with a clear vocabulary and practical methods for describing how audiovisual meaning is constructed.

## What the course explores

Throughout the course, learners are invited to move from an initial impression towards a more precise analysis of audiovisual form. They learn to identify what changes between different versions of a scene, describe the effects produced and connect those effects to specific choices involving images, sounds, editing and performance.

By working through the six modules, learners can:

* recognise that every audiovisual work results from a series of formal decisions;
* identify key choices involving editing, framing, point of view, sound and sequencing;
* analyse how images and sounds organise time, space, attention and meaning;
* distinguish between what is shown, what is heard and what viewers are invited to imagine;
* compare different constructions of the same action or situation;
* explain how formal choices influence interpretation and emotional experience;
* experiment with audiovisual analysis, annotation and production.

## Course structure

The course consists of six complementary modules:

### 1. CUT — Editing, duration and rhythm

This module examines how editing shapes the experience of time. Long takes, ellipses, matches, abrupt cuts and slow motion organise the duration and rhythm of an action in different ways.

### 2. OBSERVE — Framing, distance and point of view

This module explores how camera position, framing, shot scale and distance propose a particular place from which viewers observe a scene. Different viewpoints transform the relationship between the viewer, the characters and the action.

### 3. HEAR — Sound, silence and points of audition

This module considers how sound transforms the perception of images. Ambience, off-screen sounds, music, voice-over and bodily sounds construct space, direct attention and influence the emotional meaning of a scene.

### 4. CONNECT — Images, associations and sequencing

This module examines how meaning emerges between images. Through juxtaposition, repetition, sequence, ellipsis and visual clues, viewers connect fragments and formulate hypotheses about situations that are not fully represented.

### 5. SHOW / HIDE — Framing, off-screen space and traces

This module explores the relationship between what is visible and what remains outside the frame. Sounds, objects, empty spaces and visible consequences can make an absent event perceptible and invite viewers to reconstruct what has not been shown.

### 6. CHOOSE — Selection, variation and decision-making

The final module brings together the principles explored throughout the course. It shows how selecting, excluding, arranging and transforming audiovisual material produces different interpretations of the same situation.

## Who this course is for

The course is intended primarily for teachers, trainers, students and anyone interested in image education, audiovisual analysis or media literacy. It can also support researchers and professionals who need practical methods for discussing how audiovisual works construct meaning.

No previous specialist knowledge of film or video production is required. The concepts are introduced progressively through observation and comparison before being applied in analytical and practical activities.

## How to use the course

The modules are designed to be followed in sequence, as each one introduces a different dimension of audiovisual language and the final module brings these dimensions together. They can also be used independently when focusing on a particular topic.

Each module contains:

* a short audiovisual episode composed of five variations;
* a comparative analysis of those variations;
* explanations of the principal concepts;
* three activities moving from collective observation to annotation and production;
* suggestions for further viewing and reading.

The activities are designed primarily for use with a group or class. Their duration, level of detail and technical requirements can be adapted to the learners and the time available. Collaborative annotation activities use Celluloid, while production activities can be completed with smartphones or other readily available recording equipment.

The course does not require formal assessment. Learning develops through comparison, discussion, the formulation of interpretations and practical experimentation. The central aim is to help learners understand that audiovisual grammar is not a fixed set of rules, but a way of identifying and analysing the choices through which images and sounds construct meaning.
