## Introduction

### Observe: framing, distance, point of view

In this second module, audiovisual language is approached through an apparently simple gesture: observing. To observe is not simply to look at what is in front of us. It always means looking from a position, through a frame, with a certain distance, a certain proximity and a certain orientation of the gaze.

The episode is based on an ordinary situation: a couple assembling a piece of furniture in an otherwise empty living room. The action remains broadly the same, but it is filmed through several contrasting modes of looking. The viewer never sees exactly the same scene, even when the situation does not change.

The module therefore invites learners to distinguish between what is shown, the position from which it is shown, and the effect produced by that position of looking. Audiovisual language does not merely give access to a scene: it organises a way of observing it.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

* understand that point of view transforms the perception of a scene;
* identify several modes of looking: documentary, subjective, fragmented, object-based and selfie;
* distinguish between framing, distance, shot scale and camera position;
* analyse the effects produced by proximity, distance or fragmentation;
* use collaborative annotation to identify the moments when the position of the gaze changes the viewer’s experience.

## Watch the episode

Watch the episode once without taking notes. Pay attention to how each mode of looking changes your relationship to the same scene. From what position do we observe the action? When do you feel close to it, distant from it, immersed in it or excluded from it?

## Episode analysis

### Comparing five ways of looking

The five variations organise the same action through contrasting viewing positions: documentary, subjective, fragmented, object-based and selfie. Comparing them reveals how framing, shot scale, camera position and distance shape the viewer’s relationship to the scene. The action remains recognisable, but each visual choice offers a different way of observing it.

### Five variations

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the five variations">
  <Disclosure title="Documentary gaze">
    The scene is observed from a distance, with a static camera. The couple appears as a phenomenon to be described.

    **To observe:** Frontality, stability of the frame, distance of observation.

    **Concepts:** Static camera, wide framing, observational distance.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Subjective gaze">
    The scene is perceived from the position of one of the characters. The viewer shares a situated place within the interaction.

    **To observe:** Point-of-view shot, imperfect framing, proximity and orientation of the gaze.

    **Concepts:** Subjective viewpoint, point-of-view shot, visual immersion.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Fragmented gaze">
    The action is presented in pieces: hands, objects, faces and details. The viewer must mentally reconstruct the whole.

    **To observe:** Absence of an establishing shot, extreme close-ups and reconstruction of the action.

    **Concepts:** Fragmentation, detail, off-screen space.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Object-based gaze">
    The camera adopts the position of the box. The gaze seems to be located within the material object itself.

    **To observe:** Unusual point of view, handling and movements of the object.

    **Concepts:** Object-based viewpoint, embedded camera, unusual camera position.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Selfie gaze">
    The couple films itself with a smartphone. The scene becomes a small self-mediated event.

    **To observe:** Handheld camera, unstable framing and gaze towards the screen.

    **Concepts:** Self-representation, self-mediated image, direct address.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

### What the episode explores

* **What is shown:** The same action provides a shared basis for comparing the five variations.
* **The position of the gaze:** The camera successively occupies the position of an outside observer, a character, a fragmented viewpoint, an object or a smartphone held by the couple.
* **The effect produced:** The scene may feel distant, immersive, fragmented, strange, playful or self-conscious.

**Key takeaway**

> Audiovisual language does not simply show a scene. Through framing, distance and camera position, it proposes a place from which the viewer observes and experiences it.

## Key concepts

The following sections examine two complementary dimensions of observation. Point of view organises the viewer’s position in relation to a scene, while framing, distance and composition organise what becomes visible within the image.

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the key concepts">
  <Disclosure title="Point of view: organising a place for the viewer">
    An image is always situated. Every image is produced from a position: a distance, a height, an orientation, a frame and a relationship to bodies and objects. Point of view does not only correspond to the physical location of the camera. It also determines how the scene becomes perceptible: near or distant, intimate or external, stable or unstable, partial or overall.

    **Four dimensions of point of view**

    * **Physical point of view:** The material place from which the scene is filmed: in front, behind, above, at eye level, close to the ground, inside an object or in a character’s hand. It answers the question: Where is the camera placed?
    * **Perceptual point of view:** The way the image organises what the viewer can perceive. The frame may provide a wide view, isolate a detail, hide part of the action or direct attention. It answers the question: What does this position allow us to see or prevent us from seeing?
    * **Narrative point of view:** The relationship between what the viewer sees and what the characters know, see or ignore. It answers the question: With whom do we see, or according to what logic is the scene presented?
    * **Affective point of view:** The effect produced by the position of looking: proximity, distance, discomfort, immersion, strangeness, complicity or humour. It answers the question: What does this way of looking make us feel?

    **Point of view is not neutral**

    Changing point of view does not simply mean changing the camera angle. It changes the relationship between the viewer, the scene and the characters.

    A point of view can:

    * bring the viewer closer or create distance;
    * make an action more readable or more partial;
    * create an impression of objectivity or involvement;
    * make a scene more intimate, colder, funnier or stranger;
    * direct attention towards a gesture, an object, a detail or a whole;
    * give the viewer the position of a witness, participant or indirect observer.

    There is no completely neutral gaze. Even a static, distant and stable camera offers a position: that of an observer kept at a distance.

    **Key takeaway**

    > Point of view organises the viewer’s place. It combines camera position with choices of framing, distance, angle, shot scale, stability and fragmentation. Observing a scene therefore always means observing it from a constructed position.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Frame, distance and composition: organising what is visible">
    The frame is the boundary of the image. It determines what enters the image, what remains outside it and how visible elements are organised. Framing means selecting a portion of space: deciding what will be shown, excluded, centred, placed at the edge, brought forward or kept in the background.

    **On-screen and off-screen space**

    On-screen space is what appears within the frame. Off-screen space is what exists, or may be imagined, beyond its boundaries. A look towards the edge, a sound coming from elsewhere or a partially visible object can make this unseen space present. This relationship will be explored in greater depth in the module **SHOW / HIDE**.

    **Shot scales**

    * **Wide shot:** Shows an extended space and situates characters within an environment. It makes the overall organisation of the scene visible.
    * **Medium shot:** Shows a character or action widely enough to follow gestures and movements.
    * **Medium close-up:** Brings the viewer closer to a body, face or gesture and reinforces attention to action or expression.
    * **Close-up:** Isolates a face, hand, object or detail. It can intensify perception while fragmenting the surrounding space.

    **Composition**

    Composition is the organisation of elements within the frame: bodies, objects, lines, empty spaces, directions, depth, light and movement. It guides the viewer’s attention. An element placed in the centre does not produce the same effect as one pushed to the edge. A body isolated in a large space does not create the same impression as a body tightly framed in a narrow space.

    **Camera stability and position**

    * **Static frame:** Creates an impression of stability and observational distance. It allows the viewer to compare bodies and objects within the space.
    * **Handheld camera:** Makes the gaze more mobile, unstable and embodied. It can produce an impression of participation or presence.
    * **Mounted or situated camera:** A camera attached to, placed inside or positioned on an object strongly shifts perception. It may create a playful, strange or experimental effect.

    **Key takeaway**

    > Framing organises visible space through selection, distance and composition. Shot scale, camera stability and the arrangement of bodies and objects direct attention and shape the viewer’s relationship to the scene. Every frame shows something while leaving something else outside it.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

## Activities

These activities move from comparing viewing positions to collaborative annotation and practical filming. They invite learners to identify how framing, distance and camera position shape their relationship to the same action.

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the activities">
  <Disclosure title="Activity 1 — Same action, five viewing positions">
    > **Type:** Group analysis\
    > **Duration:** 45 minutes\
    > **Objective:** Compare the five variations of the episode to identify how framing, distance and point of view transform the perception of the same scene.

    **Materials**

    * Video of the episode *OBSERVE*
    * Projector or shared screen
    * Board or collaborative tool
    * Module concepts

    **Procedure**

    1. **Viewing without technical instructions:** Screen the episode once without interruption. Ask participants to note what changes in their viewing position: proximity, distance, immersion, detachment, fragmentation or strangeness.
    2. **Sharing impressions:** Gather the group’s impressions before introducing technical concepts. Which variation brings us closest to the action? Which creates the greatest distance? In which variation must we reconstruct what is happening?
    3. **Second viewing, variation by variation:** Pause after each variation. Build three columns: what we see / from what position we see it / what this position produces.
    4. **Collective synthesis:** Show that the frame selects, distance transforms the relationship to the action, and point of view always proposes a place for the viewer.

    > **Trainer’s role:** Start from participants’ impressions before naming the techniques: frame, point of view, distance, shot scale and point-of-view shot.
    >
    > **Variant / extension:** Divide the group into five subgroups. Each subgroup analyses one variation and presents the mode of looking identified.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Activity 2 — Where am I placed?">
    > **Type:** Collaborative annotation with Celluloid\
    > **Duration:** 1 hour\
    > **Objective:** Collectively identify the moments when the position of the gaze transforms the perception of the scene.

    **Preparation**

    Before the session, add the episode *OBSERVE* to Celluloid and create the following annotation prompt: “Annotate the moments when you feel your viewing position change. First describe your impression, then connect it to a formal choice: framing, distance, shot scale, camera position, fragmentation or filming setup.”

    **Procedure**

    1. **Launch:** Explain that the aim is not only to name a type of shot, but to describe what this position of looking produces.
    2. **Individual or pair annotation:** Each participant adds several annotations answering a simple question: Where am I placed as a viewer?
    3. **Collective reading:** Project the annotations and group them around three areas: what the frame shows or excludes; the distance between the camera and the action; the place proposed to the viewer.
    4. **Synthesis:** Ask the group to complete the sentence: “In this variation, the gaze places me…” Possible answers include at a distance, at the heart of the action or in an unusual position.

    > **Trainer’s role:** Support the shift from descriptive annotation to analysis. Encourage participants to specify what they see, from what position they see it and what effect this position produces.
    >
    > **Variant / extension:** Compare two annotations of the same moment. Ask which one best describes the viewer’s position and which one most clearly analyses the effect of framing or point of view.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Activity 3 — Filming from several points of view">
    > **Type:** Group production\
    > **Duration:** 1.5 to 2 hours\
    > **Objective:** Experiment with how framing, distance and camera position transform the perception of the same action.

    **Materials**

    * Smartphones or cameras
    * Tripods, supports or objects for stabilising the shot
    * A simple filming space
    * A few objects to handle
    * A simple editing app, if needed
    * Screen or projector for sharing the work

    **Procedure**

    1. **Choosing the action:** Each group selects a short, readable and easily repeatable action. Keep it simple so that attention remains focused on how it is filmed.
    2. **Choosing points of view:** For each version, decide where to place the camera, from what distance to film and what the frame should show or leave outside.
    3. **Filming:** Film the same action several times. The aim is not to produce a “successful” image, but to create clear differences in perception: distance, proximity, fragmentation, immersion, strangeness or direct address.
    4. **Collective viewing:** Screen the different versions. Observe what changes: what remains visible, what disappears, the place proposed to the viewer and the effect produced.

    > **Trainer’s role:** Help groups formulate their choices before filming: camera position, distance, framing and composition. During the discussion, support the shift from comparison to analysis by introducing the relevant concepts.
    >
    > **Variant / extension:** Give every group the same action, then assign each group a different viewing position. The final comparison will show more clearly how the same situation produces different experiences depending on how it is observed.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

## Further viewing and reading

The following references extend the *OBSERVE* module by examining how framing, distance, composition and viewing position shape a scene. Short extracts can be used to introduce a question, compare different approaches or provide material for analysis after the activities.

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the references">
  <Disclosure title="Classic and landmark films">
    * *The Passion of Joan of Arc* — Carl Theodor Dreyer, 1928. Close-ups and facial composition transform the viewer’s relationship to the character. **To observe:** Proximity, facial detail, intensity of attention.
    * *Citizen Kane* — Orson Welles, 1941. Depth of field and composition organise bodies, objects and settings within the frame. **To observe:** Spatial depth, visual hierarchy, relationships between foreground and background.
    * *Rear Window* — Alfred Hitchcock, 1954. The central character observes the world from a fixed and limited position. **To observe:** Distance, restricted viewpoint, the viewer’s position.
    * *Pickpocket* — Robert Bresson, 1959. Hands, gestures and fragments of action invite the viewer to reconstruct what is happening. **To observe:** Fragmentation, detail, reconstruction of action.
    * *Peeping Tom* — Michael Powell, 1960. The camera becomes a viewing apparatus within the film itself. **To observe:**&#x53;ubjective looking, intrusive gaze, relationship between seeing, filming and being seen.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Popular and contemporary films">
    * *The Blair Witch Project* — Daniel Myrick and Eduardo Sánchez, 1999. The handheld amateur-camera setup creates an immersive and unstable viewing position. **To observe:** Handheld camera, subjective looking, impression of participation.
    * *The Diving Bell and the Butterfly* — Julian Schnabel, 2007. The opening places the viewer within a constrained, partial and embodied perception. **To observe:** Point-of-view shot, restricted vision, physical and affective viewpoint.
    * *Cloverfield* — Matt Reeves, 2008. A handheld camera integrated into the action transforms the viewer into a situated witness. **To observe:** Frame instability, immersion, recording device within the scene.
    * *Son of Saul* — László Nemes, 2015. A very close frame centred on one character limits what can be seen while intensifying off-screen space. **To observe:** Restricted framing, proximity, unseen space.
    * *Searching* — Aneesh Chaganty, 2018. Screens, interfaces, webcams and digital windows organise the entire field of vision. **To observe:** Mediated looking, screen composition, recording interfaces.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Bibliography">
    * Burch, N. (1990). *Life to those shadows* (B. Brewster, Trans. & Ed.). University of California Press.\
      An important text for understanding the historical construction of audiovisual looking and how framing organises a position for the viewer.
    * Bonitzer, P. (1985). *Décadrages: Peinture et cinéma*. Cahiers du cinéma / Éditions de l’Étoile.\
      A useful reference for thinking about framing, off-screen space, looking and the effects produced by shifts or breaks in composition.
    * Pinel, V. (2005). *Vocabulaire technique du cinéma*. Armand Colin.\
      A practical tool for clarifying technical vocabulary related to framing, shot scale, camera angle and point of view.
    * Jullier, L. (2025). *L’analyse de séquences* (6e éd.). Armand Colin.\
      An accessible guide to observing a sequence precisely and connecting formal choices with the effects they produce.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Suggestions for trainers">
    * Choose two or three short, clearly readable extracts rather than taking an encyclopaedic approach.
    * Compare contrasting viewing positions instead of presenting a complete history of framing.
    * Ask learners to identify where the camera is placed, what the frame includes or excludes, and what effect this position produces.
    * Where possible, compare a classical example with a contemporary one.
    * Use the extracts as extensions of the *OBSERVE* episode rather than as fixed illustrations of a rule.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

## Conclusion

Every audiovisual image results from a set of spatial choices. Camera position, framing, shot scale, composition and stability determine which parts of a scene become accessible and how the viewer relates to them. Moving the camera or changing the distance does more than alter the appearance of an image: it changes the experience of observing.

By comparing the five variations of *OBSERVE* and experimenting through annotation or filming, learners can identify how a viewpoint is constructed and describe its effects more precisely. The next module turns from the position of the gaze to the position of listening, exploring how sounds, silences and listening perspectives shape audiovisual experience.
