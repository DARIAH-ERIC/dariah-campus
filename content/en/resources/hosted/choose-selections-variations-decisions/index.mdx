## Introduction

### Choosing: Every Form Is a Decision

In this sixth and final module, audiovisual language is approached as a process of selection and decision. Framing, cutting, arranging, repeating, showing or withholding all involve choosing between different possibilities. These choices determine what viewers notice, how they connect the images and what meaning they construct.

The episode begins with a simple shared activity: a couple sorting objects between boxes marked with a heart and a skull. The same situation is then reshaped through different selections of shots and points of attention. By bringing together framing, editing, rhythm, association and performance, the module shows that audiovisual form emerges from a succession of choices rather than from a neutral recording of events.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

* Understand that every audiovisual work results from a series of formal choices.
* Identify what has been selected, excluded, arranged, repeated or transformed.
* Analyse how different ways of filming and editing the same action affect its meaning.
* Distinguish between choices involving framing, editing, sound, rhythm and performance.
* Explain how a formal decision can guide the viewer’s attention and interpretation.

## Watch the Episode

Watch the episode once without taking notes. Observe how the same sorting activity is presented five times. In each variation, what has been selected for the viewer’s attention: the couple’s interaction, the objects, visual categories, facial expressions or the creation of new arrangements? Notice what each version includes, excludes and transforms.

## Episode Analysis

### One Activity, Five Ways of Choosing

The five variations do not simply show different outcomes of the sorting activity. Each one changes the information available to viewers and the way decisions become perceptible. A choice may appear as a shared process, a succession of objects, a visual classification, an exchange of reactions or an opportunity to create new combinations.

The episode therefore places two forms of decision in parallel. The couple chooses what to keep, reject or transform, while the audiovisual construction chooses what to show, omit, isolate, repeat and connect. Meaning emerges from the relationship between these practical and formal decisions.

### Five Variations

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the Five Variations">
  <Disclosure title="Sorting as a Shared Process">
    In a sustained two-shot, the couple takes objects from the central box one by one. They examine them, try some of them on, play with them and react to each other before deciding where they belong. The boxes marked with a heart and a skull make the alternatives visible, while the shared frame allows viewers to follow the time, gestures and interactions involved in each decision.

    **To observe:** The sustained duration, the simultaneous presence of both characters and the way their reactions influence the sorting process.

    **Concepts:** Long take, duration, shared frame, negotiation.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Sorting as a Material Inventory">
    Objects are presented individually in close-up before being placed into the box. As the sequence progresses, an overhead view shows them accumulating inside it. The couple’s faces and interactions disappear, shifting attention from the emotional process of choosing to the material result of the sorting activity.

    **To observe:** The isolation of each object, the repeated movement from table to box and the gradual accumulation seen from above.

    **Concepts:** Close-up, repetition, accumulation, ellipsis.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Selection Through Visual Series">
    The objects are reorganised into two visual series: first, small animal figures, then a succession of glasses. These fragments suggest that a selection has already taken place, although the sorting process itself remains largely unseen. The abrupt break in the soundtrack brings viewers back to the present moment, when the couple must decides what to do with the final object remaining from the series.

    **To observe:** The fragmentary series of objects, the implied earlier selection, the abrupt sound cut and the return to the final decision

    **Concepts:** Visual series, selection, ellipsis, sound cut.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Sorting Through Reactions">
    After a brief two-shot establishes the sorting activity, alternating close-ups isolate the couple’s faces. The objects and most of the sorting gestures then remain outside the frame, while changing expressions, glances and subtle movements suggest approval, hesitation, disagreement or compromise. Viewers must infer the unseen objects and the decisions being made from the exchange of reactions.

    **To observe:** The alternation between faces and the meanings produced by glances, pauses and changes in expression.

    **Concepts:** Shot/reverse shot, reaction shot, off-screen space, inference.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Sorting as Playful Recombination">
    Instead of simply deciding whether to keep or discard each object, the couple combines several of them to create temporary arrangements. Figurines are brought together, while everyday objects become supports and parts of small constructions. The sequence transforms sorting into an act of invention before the objects are returned to the box and it is closed.

    **To observe:** The unexpected associations between objects, the collaborative gestures and the progression from selection to construction.

    **Concepts:** Association, juxtaposition, transformation, visual composition.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

### What the Episode Explores

* **Choosing what to show:** Each variation selects different elements of the same activity: interaction, objects, visual series, facial expressions or playful arrangements.
* **Choosing what to leave out:** Decisions can remain partly invisible when the editing removes the sorting process, the objects or the exchanges between the characters.
* **Choosing how to connect:** Repetition, accumulation, shot/reverse shot, sound cuts and juxtaposition organise the selected fragments and guide interpretation.
* **Choosing how to transform:** Audiovisual form can turn an ordinary activity into a negotiation, an inventory, an implied earlier selection, an exchange of reactions or an act of invention.

### Key Takeaway

> Audiovisual language is made through choices. What is selected, excluded, ordered and connected determines not only what viewers see, but also how they understand and experience a scene.

## Key Concepts

The episode brings together two complementary ideas. The first examines selection as a fundamental act of audiovisual construction: every shot and edit retains certain elements while excluding others. The second explores variation, showing how different formal choices can transform the perception and meaning of the same situation.

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the Key Concepts">
  <Disclosure title="Selection and Editing: Keeping or Excluding">
    Editing involves selecting shots, excluding others and deciding their order, duration, rhythm and connections. It does not reproduce everything that was filmed but constructs a particular version from a range of possibilities. In the episode, the couple sort the objects while the audiovisual form simultaneously sorts the visible material.

    **Selection Creates Importance**

    Keeping a shot, gesture, object, expression, sound or silence gives it a place and a value within the sequence. Isolating an object in close-up does not produce the same effect as showing it within the couple’s interaction or placing it in a visual series. Selection creates a hierarchy and directs the viewer’s attention.

    **Cuts Also Tell a Story**

    A cut does more than remove material. It can condense an action, conceal part of a process or connect separate fragments. In the material inventory, the objects appear individually before accumulating inside the box. The discussion and hesitation involved in sorting them disappear, leaving viewers with a succession of objects and the visible result of earlier decisions.

    **What Is Excluded Remains Meaningful**

    In the sequence organised into visual series, only fragments of an earlier selection remain. The sorting itself is largely omitted, but its existence can be inferred from the succession of figurines and glasses. In the sequence of facial reactions, the opposite occurs: the people remain visible while the objects and gestures are excluded.

    **Editing Can Reveal or Conceal Complexity**

    A version may preserve the duration and interaction involved in making a decision, or it may show only selected objects, expressions or results. Editing therefore determines how much of the process viewers can perceive and how much they must reconstruct.

    > **Key Takeaway:** Selection gives importance to what remains while allowing excluded elements to continue shaping meaning. Editing is therefore not merely a technical operation but a way of guiding the viewer’s experience.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Formal Choices and Variation: Transforming the Same Action">
    An audiovisual scene never has only one possible form. The same action can be filmed from different distances, organised around different subjects or reconstructed through a different rhythm, soundtrack and order of shots. Each formal choice changes what viewers perceive and how they interpret the situation.

    **Filming Means Choosing a Way of Looking**

    A scene is already constructed before editing begins. Camera position, framing, distance and shot duration determine the viewer’s relationship to the action. A sustained two-shot shows the couple negotiating together, while close-ups can isolate objects or facial expressions. Each approach makes certain aspects of the situation visible and leaves others outside the frame.

    **Variation Reveals Different Dimensions of an Action**

    The five versions reorganise the same sorting activity around different centres of attention: shared interaction, material accumulation, fragmented visual series, facial reactions and playful recombination. The activity remains recognisable, but each version produces a different understanding of what choosing involves.

    **Rhythm and Sound Shape Perception**

    Repetition can turn individual objects into an inventory or a visual category. Duration can make interaction and hesitation perceptible. An abrupt sound cut can interrupt a series and return viewers to the present moment of decision. Rhythm and sound therefore influence whether choosing appears continuous, fragmented, reflective or decisive.

    **Objects Can Be Transformed Through Association**

    The meaning of an object depends on how it is framed and connected to other elements. Presented alone, it becomes an item to examine. Placed in a series, it becomes part of a category. Combined with other objects, it can become part of a temporary construction or a small imagined scene.

    **Formal Choices Are Choices of Meaning**

    Choosing a form means deciding what will become perceptible: an interaction, a material result, a visual resemblance, an emotional reaction or an inventive gesture. Form does not come after meaning as decoration; it actively participates in its construction.

    > **Key Takeaway:** Variation demonstrates that audiovisual language does not simply record an action. By changing framing, duration, rhythm, sound and association, it constructs different experiences from the same situation.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

## Activities

The following activities invite learners to identify formal decisions, compare different versions of the same material and experiment with selection themselves. They progress from collective analysis to collaborative annotation and practical audiovisual production.

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the Activities">
  <Disclosure title="Activity 1 — Same action, Five Ways of Choosing">
    > **Type:** Group analysis\
    > **Duration:** 45 minutes\
    > **Objective:** To understand how framing, editing, sound, rhythm and point of view can produce different interpretations of the same sorting activity.

    **Materials**

    * The *Choose* episode
    * A projector or shared screen
    * A board or collaborative tool
    * The module’s key concept materials

    **Instructions**

    1. **Watch the complete episode**\
       Watch the episode once without interruption. For each variation, note what attracts your attention and what you understand about the act of choosing.
    2. **Share initial impressions**\
       Compare the group’s responses before introducing technical concepts. Begin with a simple question: *How does your perception of the sorting activity change from one variation to another?*
    3. **Analyse each variation**\
       Watch the five variations again, pausing after each one. Identify:
       * what has been selected for the viewer’s attention;
       * what has been excluded or left for viewers to infer;
       * the main formal choices involving framing, editing, sound or rhythm;
       * the effect produced by those choices.
    4. **Build a collective conclusion**\
       Formulate a sentence explaining how the five versions transform the same activity. Emphasise that audiovisual form can present sorting as a shared process, a material inventory, an earlier selection suggested through fragments, an exchange of reactions or an act of playful recombination.

    > **Trainer’s Role**
    >
    > Help learners distinguish the initial activity from the formal choices that transform it. Encourage them to move from impressions to precise observations: What has been retained? What has disappeared? What has been isolated, repeated or connected? How do sound and editing guide interpretation?
    >
    > **Variant or Extension**
    >
    > Divide the learners into five groups and assign one variation to each group. Ask each group to answer three questions:
    >
    > * What is the main formal choice?
    > * What does this choice exclude or conceal?
    > * What does it make more perceptible?
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Activity 2 — Annotating Formal Choices: What Is Retained, Excluded or Transformed">
    > **Type:** Collaborative annotation with Celluloid\
    > **Duration:** 1 hour\
    > **Objective:** To identify precisely how formal choices transform the perception of the same activity.

    **Preparation**

    Before the session, import the *Choose* episode into Celluloid and add the following annotation prompt: "*Identify moments when a formal choice transforms your perception of the sorting activity. First describe what you see or hear, then identify the technique used and explain its effect".*

    **Instructions**

    1. **Introduce the activity**\
       Explain that the aim is not simply to comment on the couple’s actions, but to observe how audiovisual form organises them. Briefly recall the five approaches: shared interaction, material inventory, selection suggested through visual series, facial reactions and playful recombination.
    2. **Annotate individually or in pairs**\
       Add several annotations to the video. Each annotation should answer three questions:
       * What can I see or hear?
       * Which formal choice can I identify?
       * How does it affect my perception of the sorting activity?
    3. **Compare and organise the annotations**\
       Display the annotations and group them into categories such as:
       * framing and camera distance;
       * shot duration and cuts;
       * inclusion and exclusion;
       * repetition and visual series;
       * sound and sound cuts;
       * point of view and reaction shots;
       * association and visual composition.
    4. **Summarise each variation**\
       Formulate a short collective conclusion explaining which elements each variation retains, excludes and transforms.

    > **Trainer’s Role**
    >
    > Help learners move from an impression to an identifiable technique. Ask what has been shown, omitted, isolated, repeated or connected, and what the editing makes more perceptible or leaves viewers to infer.
    >
    > **Variant or Extension**
    >
    > Compare two annotations referring to the same moment. Discuss whether the formal choice produces a shared interpretation or different responses. This comparison demonstrates that form can strongly guide meaning while still leaving room for individual interpretation.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Activity 3 — Filming One Decision, Editing Several Versions">
    > **Type:** Group production\
    > **Duration:** 1 hour 30 minutes to 2 hours\
    > **Objective:** To experiment with how choices involving filming, editing, sound and rhythm can transform the same decision-making activity.

    **Materials**

    * Smartphones or cameras
    * Tripods, supports or objects for stabilising the camera
    * A simple filming location
    * A selection of everyday objects
    * A basic editing application
    * A screen or projector for presenting the productions

    **Instructions**

    1. **Choose an action**\
       Each group selects a simple decision-making activity that can be performed quickly—for example, sorting books, choosing between two photographs, emptying a bag, deciding which objects should remain on a table or dividing belongings between two boxes.
    2. **Plan the versions**\
       Define two or three different formal treatments. For each version, decide:
       * what will be shown;
       * what will be excluded;
       * which elements will receive the most attention;
       * the dominant rhythm;
       * the role of direct sound, silence or music;
       * the intended effect on viewers.
    3. **Film the material**\
       Film the activity several times or create a shared collection of shots that can be edited differently. Include simple shots of hands, objects, faces, gestures, glances, pauses and changes in position.
    4. **Edit the versions**\
       Create several versions by modifying the duration and order of shots, the presence or absence of hesitation, the balance between objects and faces, the soundtrack, repetitions or sound cuts.
    5. **Present and compare**\
       Screen the versions without explaining the intended effects beforehand. Viewers first describe what they noticed, understood and inferred. The group then explains the formal choices used to guide those interpretations.

    > **Trainer’s Role**
    >
    > Help groups avoid relying on explanatory dialogue or exaggerated acting. Encourage them to produce meaning through framing, duration, editing, sound, repetition and association. During the discussion, identify what each version retains, excludes, rearranges and transforms.
    >
    > **Variant or Extension**
    >
    > Give every group the same collection of shots. Each group creates a different version by choosing its own order, rhythm, soundtrack and centre of attention. Comparing the results makes the role of editing as an act of selection and decision particularly clear.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

## Further Viewing and Reading

The following films and readings extend the module’s exploration of selection, variation and formal decision-making. They demonstrate how audiovisual works construct meaning by choosing what to retain, exclude, repeat, rearrange or transform.

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the References">
  <Disclosure title="Classic and Landmark Films">
    François Truffaut, *Day for Night*, 1973\
    A useful film for showing that audiovisual production involves a succession of practical and formal decisions: framing, repeating a take, cutting, adapting performances and resolving questions of rhythm or continuity.

    Frederick Wiseman, *High School*, 1968\
    A powerful example of documentary editing as selection. Without direct commentary, the film constructs a point of view through what it retains, arranges and connects.

    Krzysztof Kieślowski, *Blind Chance*, 1987\
    The film develops several possible trajectories from the same starting point. It demonstrates how one event, gesture or instant can generate different narrative outcomes.

    Alain Resnais, *Smoking/No Smoking*, 1993\
    A particularly relevant work for exploring the consequences of a seemingly minor choice. The narrative unfolds through multiple variations generated by different decisions.

    Hirokazu Kore-eda, *After Life*, 1998\
    The characters must select a single memory to preserve. The film invites reflection on selection, memory and attachment, as well as on the staging of a remembered experience.

    Jørgen Leth and Lars von Trier, *The Five Obstructions*, 2003\
    The same film is remade several times under different constraints. This provides a particularly clear demonstration of how imposed formal choices can profoundly transform the same source material.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Popular and Contemporary Films">
    Tom Tykwer, *Run Lola Run*, 1998\
    An accessible example of variation: the same situation is repeated with different events, choices and narrative consequences.

    Peter Howitt, *Sliding Doors*, 1998\
    A minor event produces two parallel trajectories. The film offers a straightforward introduction to narrative variation and alternative possibilities.

    Lee Unkrich, *Toy Story 3*, 2010\
    A useful popular reference for exploring objects, attachment, sorting and the decisions involved in keeping, passing on or abandoning possessions.

    Richard Ayoade, *Submarine*, 2010\
    The film shows how a subjective point of view can transform ordinary events into mental projections and imagined scenarios.

    Jim Jarmusch, *Paterson*, 2016\
    Repeated gestures and subtle variations give value to everyday life. The film demonstrates how small choices of attention can transform apparently ordinary actions.

    Daniel Kwan and Daniel Scheinert, *Everything Everywhere All at Once*, 2022\
    A contemporary example of multiplying possibilities, bifurcations and alternative versions of the same existence.

    Wim Wenders, *Perfect Days*, 2023\
    Repeated routines and discreet variations transform simple everyday actions into a sensitive experience through framing, duration, sound and performance.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Bibliography">
    Villain, D. (1991). *Le montage au cinéma*. Cahiers du cinéma.\
    A clear introduction to editing as an operation of selection, organisation and transformation of meaning.

    Reisz, K., & Millar, G. (1968). *The technique of film editing* (2nd ed.). Focal Press.\
    A classic reference on editing decisions, continuity, ellipsis, rhythm and the construction of scenes.

    Dancyger, K. (2026). *The technique of film and video editing: History, theory, and practice* (7th ed.). Routledge.\
    A comprehensive resource connecting technical editing decisions with their narrative, emotional and rhythmic effects.

    Pearlman, K. (2009). *Cutting rhythms: Shaping the film edit*. Focal Press.\
    A useful study of editing through rhythm, movement, bodily perception and the experience of time.

    Orpen, V. (2003). *Film editing: The art of the expressive*. Wallflower Press.\
    An accessible work explaining how editing does more than assemble shots: it functions as an expressive strategy involving image, sound, performance and mise-en-scène.

    Gaudreault, A., & Jost, F. (2017). *Le récit cinématographique: Films et séries télévisées* (3rd ed.). Armand Colin.\
    A valuable resource for understanding how audiovisual works organise information, narration, temporality and the viewer’s position.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Suggestions for Trainers">
    * Select short excerpts in which formal decisions are clearly perceptible. Begin with a simple question: *What has this sequence chosen to retain, exclude or transform?*
    * Compare contrasting strategies: preserving a shared action in a sustained shot, isolating objects, arranging fragments into a visual series, withholding objects to emphasise facial reactions, or creating meaning through unexpected associations.

    - Encourage learners to connect each effect to an identifiable choice involving framing, duration, editing, sound, repetition or point of view. The aim is to demonstrate that choosing is not only a narrative theme: it is the foundation of audiovisual construction.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

## Conclusion

This module has shown that audiovisual form is created through a succession of decisions. By selecting different shots, sounds, durations, points of view and relationships between objects, the five variations transform the same sorting activity into distinct viewing experiences.

As the final module in the course, *Choose* also brings together the principles explored throughout the six episodes. The six modules — *CUT, OBSERVE, HEAR, CONNECT, SHOW / HIDE* and *CHOOSE* — all involve selecting among possibilities. Understanding audiovisual grammar therefore means learning to identify these choices, analyse their effects and experiment with alternative ways of constructing meaning.
