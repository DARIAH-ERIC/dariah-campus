## Introduction

### Cutting: editing as a way of shaping time

In this first module, audiovisual language is approached through a fundamental operation: cutting. To cut is not simply to remove part of an action or to move from one shot to another. It means organising time: deciding what remains visible, what disappears, what connects to what, and what is compressed or stretched.

The episode is based on a very simple situation: a couple unpacking boxes in an apartment. The action is ordinary, but it is repeated several times through different editing approaches. The module therefore invites learners to distinguish between real time, filmed time and perceived time. Audiovisual language does not merely record time: it shapes it.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

* understand that editing constructs an experience of time;
* identify several ways of structuring time: long take, ellipsis, match, hard cut and slow motion;
* distinguish between actual continuity and the impression of continuity;
* analyse the effects produced by cuts on the viewer’s perception;
* put into words sensations of duration, interruption, acceleration or slowing down;
* use collaborative annotation to identify the moments when time shifts.

## Episode analysis

### Comparing five experiences of time

The five variations organise the same action through contrasting temporal forms: continuous, hollowed-out, gapped, smoothed and stretched time. Comparing them reveals how long takes, ellipses, abrupt cuts, continuity matches and slow motion shape the viewer’s attention and perception of duration. The action remains recognisable, but each editing choice creates a different temporal experience.

### Five variations

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore five variations">
  <Disclosure title="Continuous time">
    The first variation shows the action in apparent continuity. It establishes a point of reference: the viewer observes an ordinary duration.&#x20;

    **To observe:** Continuity of the action, the impression of real duration, and the role of objects as markers of time.&#x20;

    **Concepts:** Long take, duration, continuity.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Hollowed-out time">
    Fragments of time disappear. The action remains understandable, but the viewer must mentally reconstruct what is missing.

    **To observe:** Changes of state, missing segments, and what is understood without being shown.

    **Concepts:** Ellipsis, cut, the viewer’s reconstruction
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Gapped time">
    The cuts become visible and abrupt. The action moves forward in jumps: time is condensed and punctured.

    **To observe:** Hard cuts, temporal jumps, and the effects of acceleration and discontinuity.

    **Concepts:** Hard cut, jump cut, fragmented rhythm.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Smoothed time">
    The action is fragmented into several shots, but the cuts are made discreet through smooth continuity matches.

    **To observe:** Gestures or objects that ensure continuity and create an impression of fluidity.

    **Concepts:** Match, invisible editing, classical continuity.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Stretched time">
    Ordinary gestures are slowed down. The viewer perceives the gesture’s duration, texture and presence.

    **To observe:** Slowing down, an impression of suspension, and the new value given to the ordinary.

    **Concepts:** Slow motion, temporal dilation, attention to gesture.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

### What the episode explores

* **The time of the action:** The time needed to unpack boxes, move objects and organise the space.
* **The time created by editing:** The time constructed through the arrangement of shots: what is kept, removed, connected, accelerated or slowed down.
* **The time perceived by the viewer:** The time felt by the viewer: shorter, longer, more fluid, more fragmented or denser.

> **Audiovisual language does not reproduce time as it is. It creates a temporal experience.**

## Key concepts

The following two sections examine how editing constructs audiovisual time. The first introduces the role of shots, cuts, duration and rhythm. The second focuses on matches and ellipses: two complementary operations that connect moments or make part of the action disappear.

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the key concepts">
  <Disclosure title="Editing: constructing audiovisual time">
    Editing is one of the fundamental operations of audiovisual language. It consists of arranging shots in a given order, with a given duration and according to a given rhythm. To edit is therefore not simply to assemble images. It is to construct an experience of time: deciding what will be shown, what will be removed, what will be extended, accelerated, slowed down or made perceptible through a cut.

    **Shots, cuts, rhythm and duration**

    * **The shot:** A continuous portion of image and sound situated between two cuts. The shot is not only an image: it is also a unit of duration.
    * **The cut:** The transition from one shot to another. It determines what we see, what disappears and what we reconstruct.
    * **Duration:** The length of a shot or a scene. It directs attention: an extended shot leaves time to observe; a brief shot can create speed or fragmentation.
    * **Rhythm:** The perceptible organisation of durations, cuts, repetitions, pauses, accelerations and slowdowns.

    **Continuity and discontinuity**

    Editing may seek to create an impression of continuity. The cuts are then made discreet through matches: a gesture begun in one shot continues in the next, an object carries the transition, or a direction remains consistent.

    Editing can also embrace discontinuity. The cuts then become more visible: the action jumps from one moment to another, time becomes fragmented, and transitions become sharper. These two approaches should not be understood as a good and a bad way of editing. They simply produce different effects.

    **What editing does to the viewer**

    Editing guides attention. It can show a gesture in its continuity, omit a moment, emphasise a detail, speed up an action, slow down a perception or make a rupture felt.

    It can lead viewers to:

    * believe they are watching a continuous action;
    * understand that a segment of time has disappeared;
    * accept an ellipsis without noticing it;
    * feel an acceleration or a rupture;
    * give more importance to an ordinary gesture;
    * mentally reconstruct what has not been shown.

    **Key takeaway**

    > Editing is a way of shaping time. It organises shots, durations, cuts and rhythms in order to construct an audiovisual experience. It does not simply reproduce the time of an action: it selects it, transforms it and makes it perceptible. Understanding editing means understanding that the time of a video is always constructed.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Matches and ellipses: holding time together, making time disappear">
    In editing, not all cuts produce the same effect. Some make the transition from one shot to another smooth, almost imperceptible. Others make us feel that a segment of time has disappeared, or that the action moves forward by leaps. This section distinguishes two essential operations: the match, which holds two shots together, and the ellipsis, which removes a segment of time while allowing the viewer to understand what happened.

    **The match: organising continuity**

    A match is a connection between two shots. It allows the viewer to move from one image to another without losing track of the action, the space or the movement. A match does not make the cut disappear. It makes it acceptable, easy to follow and sometimes almost invisible. It guides the viewer by providing points of reference: where they are, what is happening and how the shots are connected.

    **Some types of matches**

    * **Match on action:** A movement begins in one shot and continues in the next. The viewer’s attention follows the gesture.
    * **Directional match:** A movement keeps a consistent direction from one shot to the next. It helps maintain the clarity of space.
    * **Object match:** An object acts as a link between two shots: held, put down, moved or put away. It becomes an anchor point for the gaze.
    * **Sound bridge:** A sound carries across the cut: ambient noise, rubbing, a voice, cardboard or an object being set down. It can make the cut less visible.

    **Invisible editing**

    When matches work smoothly, the viewer may have the impression that the scene unfolds naturally. The cuts exist, but they do not present themselves as ruptures. The term invisible editing is sometimes used for editing that seeks to go unnoticed in favour of the continuity of the action. This does not mean that editing is neutral: it strongly organises perception, but without drawing attention to itself.

    **The ellipsis: removing so the viewer can infer**

    An ellipsis consists of removing a segment of time. The viewer does not see everything that happens, but can infer it through clues provided by the editing: a box is full in one shot, then empty in the next. The ellipsis does not merely create a shortcut. It activates the viewer, who has to reconstruct what is missing.

    * **Discreet ellipsis:** It removes part of the time without significantly disrupting comprehension.
    * **Visible ellipsis:** It makes the temporal jump felt: something is missing between two moments.
    * **Hard cut:** A direct cut, with no gradual transition. It can create a strong rupture.
    * **Jump cut:** A cut that creates a perceptible discontinuity within the same action or the same space.

    **Holding time together, making time disappear**

    **Holding time together:** The match connects shots. It creates an impression of continuity, coherence or fluidity.

    **Making time disappear:** The ellipsis removes part of time. It creates an absence that the viewer has to fill in.

    **Key takeaway**

    > A match allows two shots to hold together. An ellipsis allows a segment of time to disappear. Editing therefore constructs time through a double movement: it connects and it removes. It organises what the viewer sees, but also what they understand without having seen it. Analysing editing means observing both the links between shots and the absences between moments.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

## Activities

The following activities are designed for use with a group or class. They move progressively from shared observation to collaborative annotation and audiovisual production. Each activity can be adapted to the learners’ level, the available time and the technical equipment.

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the activities">
  <Disclosure title="Activity 1 — Comparing the five variations">
    > **Type:** Group analysis\
    > **Duration:** 45 minutes\
    > **Objective:** Compare the five variations of the episode to identify how editing transforms the perception of time.

    **Materials**

    * Video of the episode *CUT*
    * Projector or shared screen
    * Board or collaborative tool
    * Module concepts

    **Procedure**

    1. **Viewing without technical instructions:** Screen the episode without interruption. Ask participants to note an impression of duration, fluidity, acceleration, rupture or slowing down.
    2. **Sharing impressions:** Gather reactions without immediately introducing technical concepts. Which variation feels long, fast, fluid, gapped or slowed down?
    3. **Second viewing, variation by variation:** Pause after each variation. Build three columns: what I see / what I feel / what produces this effect.
    4. **Collective synthesis:** Gradually introduce the concepts of long take, cut, ellipsis, match, rhythm and slow motion. Formulate a synthesis sentence on editing and time.

    > **Trainer’s role:** Start from participants’ impressions before naming the techniques. Help them move from felt response to analysis, without looking too quickly for the right answer.
    >
    > **Variant / extension:** Divide the group into five subgroups. Each subgroup analyses one variation and then presents the temporal regime identified.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Activity 2 — Collaborative annotation with Celluloid">
    > **Type:** Collaborative annotation with Celluloid\
    > **Duration:** 1 hour\
    > **Objective:** Collectively identify the moments when editing transforms the perception of time: cut, ellipsis, match, rupture or slow motion.

    **Preparation**

    Before the session, add the episode *CUT* to Celluloid and create the following annotation prompt: “Annotate the moments when you feel time changing. First describe your impression, then connect it to an editing choice.”

    **Procedure**

    1. **Launch:** Present the objective. The aim is not only to spot cuts, but to describe their effects on the perception of time.
    2. **Individual or pair annotation:** Each participant adds several annotations: ellipsis, smooth match, visible cut, rhythmic effect or slowed gesture.
    3. **Collective reading:** Project the annotations and group them around three questions: What connects? What disappears? What effect does this produce?
    4. **Synthesis:** Ask the group to complete the sentence: “In this episode, editing transforms time into…” Possible ideas include continuity, jump, acceleration, suspension or reconstruction.

    > **Trainer’s role:** Support the shift from descriptive annotation to analysis. Encourage participants not simply to write “there is a cut”, but to specify what this cut produces.
    >
    > **Variant / extension:** Compare two annotations of the same moment: one descriptive and one analytical. Identify which one best helps to understand the editing effect.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Activity 3 — Editing time through production">
    > **Type:** Group production\
    > **Duration:** 1.5 to 2 hours\
    > **Objective:** Experiment with editing as a construction of time by filming a simple action, then producing several edited versions.

    **Materials**

    * Smartphones or lightweight cameras
    * Tripod or stable support, if possible
    * Simple editing software or app
    * Easy-to-handle objects: books, chairs, bags, sheets of paper or boxes
    * Projection screen for sharing the work

    **Procedure**

    1. **Preparation:** In groups of three or four, choose a simple action: clearing a table, emptying a bag, moving chairs, sorting sheets of paper or setting up an object.
    2. **Filming:** Film the action by planning clear shots, a few gestures that can be matched, moments that can be removed, and objects that mark the passing of time.
    3. **Editing:** Create at least two versions: a smooth version, in which the action appears to unfold naturally, and a gapped version, in which ellipses or ruptures are perceptible.
    4. **Screening and discussion:** Present the versions to the group. Which one seems fluid? Which one makes the ellipses perceptible? What does editing change in the perception of the action ?

    > **Trainer’s role:** Remind participants that the aim is not to tell a complex story or assess technical quality, but to produce perceptible differences between the versions.
    >
    > **Variant / extension:** If time allows, add a third, dilated version. Slow down or extend certain gestures to modify the viewer’s attention.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

## Further viewing and reading

The following references extend the work carried out in the *CUT* module. The films and texts listed here are not models to imitate, but points of comparison. How do other works construct time through editing, cuts, matches, ellipses, repetitions or slow motion?

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the references">
  <Disclosure title="Classic and landmark films">
    * *Man with a Movie Camera* — Dziga Vertov, 1929. Editing organises the gaze, urban rhythm, gestures and machines. **To observe:** Rhythmic editing, repetition, acceleration.
    * *Battleship Potemkin* — Sergei Eisenstein, 1925. A reference for understanding editing as dramatic intensification. **To observe:** Fragmentation, build-up of tension, montage of attractions.
    * *Sherlock Jr.* — Buster Keaton, 1924. Cuts transform space, time and the continuity of the action. **To observe:** Breaks in continuity, surprise, visual gag.
    * *À bout de souffle* — Jean-Luc Godard, 1960. The jump cut makes the cut visible and changes the rhythm of the scene. **To observe:** Discontinuity, acceleration, visible cut.
    * *La Jetée* — Chris Marker, 1962. A film built from still images, duration and voice. **To observe:** Mental time, sequencing, memory.
    * *Jeanne Dielman, 23 quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles* — Chantal Akerman, 1975. Duration and repetition transform the perception of ordinary gestures. **To observe:** Duration, everyday gestures, tiny variations.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Popular and contemporary films">
    * *Rocky* — John G. Avildsen, 1976. The training sequences condense time and build a progression. **To observe:** Ellipsis, musical rhythm, transformation.
    * *Goodfellas* — Martin Scorsese, 1990. Movement and cutting produce a spectacular continuity. **To observe:** Fluidity, sequencing, internal rhythm.
    * *Requiem for a Dream* — Darren Aronofsky, 2000. A fragmented, repetitive editing style condenses gestures. **To observe:**&#x52;epetition, fast editing, saturation.
    * *Shaun of the Dead* — Edgar Wright, 2004. Ordinary gestures are transformed by quick, comic cuts. **To observe:** Comic ellipsis, hard cut, everyday actions.
    * *Whiplash* — Damien Chazelle, 2014. Editing connects sound, gesture and bodily tension. **To observe:** Rhythm, gesture-linked cut, tension.
    * *Mad Max: Fury Road* — George Miller, 2015. Dense action editing remains highly readable. **To observe:** Guided gaze, speed, readability.
    * *Dunkirk* — Christopher Nolan, 2017. Editing links several temporalities within a single structure. **To observe:** Cross-cutting, tension, multiple temporalities.
  </Disclosure>

## Conclusion

Editing does not simply assemble shots or shorten an action. It constructs an experience of time by selecting what remains visible, removing certain moments, connecting others and organising durations into a rhythm. Through cuts, matches, ellipses, continuity and discontinuity, viewers perceive an action as fluid, fragmented, accelerated, suspended or extended.

By analysing the five variations of the *CUT* episode and experimenting through annotation or production, learners can move from an impression of time to a more precise understanding of the editing choices that create it. The next module shifts attention from time to framing, distance and point of view: the ways in which audiovisual language organises how we observe a scene.
