## Introduction

### Showing and hiding: frame, off-screen space and traces

In this fifth module, audiovisual language is explored through a fundamental tension between showing and hiding. Every image selects a portion of space and leaves something beyond the frame. What remains unseen may nevertheless become perceptible through sounds, objects, empty spaces and visible effects.

The episode explores the couple’s everyday life and relationship without showing the central events directly. Viewers infer situations from traces and consequences, reconstructing actions that remain outside the frame. This module examines how audiovisual language can make an absence perceptible and invite viewers to distinguish between what is visible, suggested and imagined.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

* Understand how what remains unseen can contribute to audiovisual meaning.
* Identify how framing separates on-screen and off-screen space.
* Analyse how traces and clues allow viewers to reconstruct a situation.
* Distinguish between an event that is shown, suggested or imagined.
* Identify the effects produced by absence, empty space, sound and objects.

## Watch the episode

Watch the episode once without taking notes. Pay attention to the moments when you understand that something has happened without seeing the event itself. Which sounds, objects, empty spaces, movements or visible consequences guide your interpretation? What do you infer, and what remains uncertain?

## Episode analysis

### An absent event, several ways of making it present

Across five variations, the episode shifts attention away from the central events and towards the signs that surround them. A prepared dinner, an unanswered message, changing lights in empty rooms, a packed suitcase and paired everyday objects allow viewers to infer different moments in the couple’s relationship.

Each variation organises these clues differently. Meaning emerges through the succession of images and through changes in objects, spaces, sounds and light. Viewers must connect what they see and hear in order to reconstruct actions that remain outside the frame.

### Five variations

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the five variations">
  <Disclosure title="An intimate encounter">
    A plate gradually fills with appetisers, while glimpses of a shower, make-up, jewellery and clothing suggest that the couple are preparing for an intimate evening. A speaker, a candle and two glasses of wine complete the setting. The food and wine then gradually disappear, leaving only the traces of a shared moment that is never shown directly.

    **To observe:** The sequence of preparatory gestures and the transformation of the objects before and after the unseen encounter.

    **Concepts:** Off-screen space, ellipsis, trace, suggestion.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Frustrated waiting">
    An unanswered message asking “Where are you?” establishes the absence of one partner. The person waiting looks through photographs of the couple, repeatedly spins a small object and manipulates a potted plant with different tools. These repetitive gestures make the passing of time and growing frustration perceptible without showing the absent person.

    **To observe:** The unanswered message, the photographs and the increasingly repetitive gestures.

    **Concepts:** Absence, duration, repetition, off-screen presence.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="An off-screen argument">
    The camera moves through empty areas of the apartment while changes in light, doors and off-screen sounds suggest a conflict taking place elsewhere. No bodies or faces are shown. The tension occupies the domestic space and must be reconstructed from what can be heard and from the changing atmosphere of the rooms.

    **To observe:** The empty rooms, variations in light and sounds coming from beyond the frame.

    **Concepts:** Off-screen sound, empty frame, suggestion, spatial tension.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="A silent separation and departure">
    Clothes gradually fill a suitcase, which is then left by the doorway. Empty hangers, an overturned chair, a single set of keys and changes among the couple’s everyday belongings suggest that one partner has left. The separation is revealed through a before-and-after sequence rather than through a farewell scene.

    **To observe:** The disappearance of clothes and the objects that indicate a newly empty place.

    **Concepts:** Trace, ellipsis, absence, visual inference.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="A reconciliation">
    A second set of keys appears beside the first, two cups are placed together and the couple’s phones rest on top of one another. Light returns to the apartment and a door opens. These small changes suggest that a shared presence has been restored, although the reunion itself remains outside the frame.

    **To observe:** The return of paired objects, the changing light and the opening door.

    **Concepts:** Visual clue, off-screen event, restored presence, suggestion.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

### What the episode explores

* **What is shown:** Peripheral details such as objects, gestures, doors, lights and changing domestic spaces.
* **What remains unseen:** The central moments in the couple’s relationship, including the encounter, waiting, conflict, departure and reunion.
* **What viewers reconstruct:** The events and emotions connecting the visible clues.

### Key takeaway

> Audiovisual meaning is not produced only by what an image reveals. What remains outside the frame can become perceptible through sounds, traces, changes and the connections made by the viewer.

## Key concepts

The episode introduces two complementary ways of thinking about the unseen. The first concerns the relationship between on-screen and off-screen space created by framing. The second examines how traces and clues enable viewers to infer events that are not directly represented.

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the key concepts">
  <Disclosure title="Off-screen space: making the unseen present">
    Off-screen space refers to everything that remains outside the visible frame but continues to exist through space, sound or the viewer’s imagination. An image never shows everything: it selects, separates and excludes. What lies beyond its boundaries can nevertheless influence how the visible elements are understood.

    **The frame as a boundary**

    The frame determines what is included in the image and what is left outside it. However, excluded elements do not necessarily disappear from the scene. A closed door, an empty room, a change in light or a sound coming from elsewhere can suggest that an event is taking place beyond the visible space. Framing therefore organises both what is revealed and what remains hidden.

    **Engaging the viewer’s imagination**

    When an event is not represented directly, viewers must complete the missing information. They connect visible details, interpret sounds and imagine what may be happening elsewhere or between two shots. Off-screen space therefore gives viewers an active role: they construct a possible situation from incomplete evidence.

    **Using sound to extend the visible space**

    Off-screen space is not exclusively visual. Voices, footsteps, a closing door or noises coming from another room can make an unseen space perceptible. Sound extends the frame by providing information about the environment surrounding the image. A conflict, for example, can become understandable without showing the people involved.

    **Showing the edges of an event**

    Audiovisual language can focus on what surrounds an event rather than on the event itself. In the episode, the camera records preparations for an intimate evening, an unanswered message, empty rooms and the objects left after a departure. These peripheral elements allow the central situations to be understood through their context and consequences.

    > **Key takeaway:** Hiding does not necessarily remove meaning. By combining framing, sound and peripheral details, audiovisual language can turn the unseen into an active space for interpretation.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Traces and clues: reconstructing an absent event">
    A trace is a visible consequence left by an action, a presence or an event. A clue is a detail that leads viewers towards a possible interpretation. Both allow an unseen situation to be reconstructed without providing a complete explanation.

    **Traces: what remains after an action**

    A trace does not represent the event itself but preserves evidence of it. In the episode, food disappearing from plates suggests that a dinner has taken place, while an open suitcase, empty hangers and a set of keys indicate a departure. Ordinary objects become meaningful because their appearance, position or number has changed.

    **Clues: details that guide interpretation**

    A clue suggests a possible explanation without confirming it. An unanswered message may indicate worry or frustration. Empty rooms and changing lights may suggest a conflict taking place elsewhere. Two sets of keys and two cups can imply that a shared presence has returned. Viewers connect these details and formulate their own hypotheses.

    **Objects as carriers of emotion**

    Objects are not merely decorative. Depending on how they are framed, arranged, repeated or removed, they can acquire an emotional meaning. A single object may evoke absence, while two similar objects placed together may suggest intimacy or reconciliation. Their meaning arises from the relationships created between them.

    **Empty space as a sign**

    An empty space can express waiting, separation, loneliness or the possibility of someone’s return. When the camera holds on an unoccupied room, a closed door or a silent entrance, the absence itself becomes perceptible. Emptiness is therefore not a lack of information but an active component of the scene.

    **Building meaning through accumulation**

    An unseen event is rarely reconstructed from a single detail. Meaning develops through accumulation: an object, a sound, an empty space and then a change in the setting. Each element provides partial information, and their combination allows viewers to imagine a possible event.

    > **Key takeaway:** Traces and clues transform visible details into signs. By connecting them, viewers actively reconstruct events that the images never show directly.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

## Activities

The following activities invite learners to explore how framing, off-screen space, traces and clues can shape meaning. They progress from observation and interpretation to collaborative annotation and practical audiovisual experimentation.

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the activities">
  <Disclosure title="Understanding without seeing">
    > **Type:** Group analysis\
    > **Duration:** 45 minutes\
    > **Objective:** To understand how a scene can produce meaning without directly showing its central event.

    **Materials**

    * The *Show / Hide* episode
    * A projector or shared screen
    * A board or collaborative tool
    * The module’s key concept materials

    **Instructions**

    1. **Watch without technical guidance**\
       Watch the complete episode without interruption. For each variation, note what you think is happening, even though the central event is not shown.
    2. **Share initial interpretations**\
       Compare the group’s impressions before introducing technical concepts. Begin with a simple question: *What did you understand without seeing it directly?*
    3. **Watch each variation again**\
       Pause after every variation. Identify:
       * what is visible;
       * what remains off-screen;
       * which visual or sound clues allow the situation to be reconstructed.
    4. **Build a collective conclusion**\
       Formulate a sentence explaining how the unseen event becomes perceptible. Emphasise that off-screen space is not simply empty: traces, objects, sounds, absences and subtle changes in the setting can make it the central location of the action.

    > **Role of the facilitator**
    >
    > Help learners distinguish between what is actually represented and what they infer from the clues. Encourage them to justify each interpretation: Which elements support it? What remains unseen? Why does the absent event nevertheless feel present?
    >
    > **Variation**
    >
    > Divide the learners into five groups and assign one variation to each group. Ask them to identify the clues through which the off-screen event becomes perceptible. Compare the different strategies during the group discussion.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Annotating off-screen space: understanding without seeing">
    > **Type:** Collaborative annotation with Celluloid\
    > **Duration:** 1 hour\
    > **Objective:** To identify moments when viewers understand a situation although the central event is not shown directly.

    **Preparation**

    Before the session, import the *Show / Hide* episode into Celluloid and add the following annotation prompt: "*Identify moments when something is understood without being shown directly. Describe the visible or audible clue, then explain what you imagine, infer or reconstruct from it".*

    **Instructions**

    1. **Introduce the activity**\
       Explain that the aim is not simply to describe what appears in the image, but to identify what the image makes perceptible beyond the frame. Briefly recall the five variations: an intimate encounter, frustrated waiting, an off-screen argument, a silent separation and departure, and a reconciliation.
    2. **Annotate individually or in pairs**\
       Add several annotations to the video. Each annotation should answer three questions:
       * What can I see or hear?
       * What remains off-screen?
       * What does this clue lead me to understand?
    3. **Compare and organise the annotations**\
       Display the annotations and group them into categories such as:
       * material traces;
       * off-screen sounds;
       * empty spaces;
       * missing objects;
       * signs of presence or absence.
    4. **Summarise each variation**\
       Formulate a short collective conclusion explaining how the unseen event becomes perceptible in each variation.

    > **Role of the facilitator**
    >
    > Help learners distinguish between three levels: what is visible or audible, what remains outside the frame, and what viewers reconstruct. Ask which clue supports each hypothesis and whether the resulting interpretation is clear, ambiguous or open.
    >
    > **Variation**
    >
    > Compare two annotations referring to the same moment. Discuss whether the learners have interpreted the same clue differently. This comparison demonstrates that off-screen space does not always produce a single meaning but can create an open space for interpretation.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Filming around an event">
    > **Type:** Group production\
    > **Duration:** 1 hour 30 minutes to 2 hours\
    > **Objective:** To experiment with making an event understandable without showing it directly, using framing, off-screen space, traces, sounds and visual clues.

    **Materials**

    * Smartphones or cameras
    * Tripods, supports or objects for stabilising the camera
    * A simple filming location
    * A selection of everyday objects
    * A basic editing application, if necessary
    * A screen or projector for presenting the productions

    **Instructions**

    1. **Choose an event**\
       Each group selects a simple event. The event itself must remain off-screen and must not be filmed directly.
    2. **Identify the clues**\
       Decide which elements will allow viewers to understand the situation: objects, sounds, lights, empty spaces, traces or indirect gestures. Determine what will be visible and what will remain hidden.
    3. **Film the sequence**\
       Organise the shots so that they progressively guide the viewer’s interpretation. The sequence may use waiting, repetition, silence, off-screen sound or the gradual appearance of a clue.
    4. **Present and discuss the productions**\
       Screen each version without explaining its intended event beforehand. Viewers first describe what they think has happened. The group that created the sequence then explains how its choices of framing, clues and off-screen space guided that interpretation.

    > **Role of the facilitator**
    >
    > Help groups avoid showing the event too directly. Encourage them to film its edges and consequences: thresholds, doors, objects, traces, empty spaces and off-screen sounds. During the discussion, distinguish between what is shown, what is hidden and what viewers reconstruct.
    >
    > **Variation**
    >
    > Give every group the same event—for example, *someone has left*. Each group must find a different way of making it understandable without showing it directly. Compare the strategies used: a material trace, an empty space, a missing object, a sound, a change in light, silence or a repeated gesture.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

## Further viewing and reading

The following films and readings provide further examples of how audiovisual works use framing, off-screen space, sound, traces and absence to make unseen events perceptible.

<Callout kind="none" title="Explore the references">
  <Disclosure title="Classic and landmark films">
    Tourneur, J. (Director). (1942). *Cat People* \[Film].\
    An essential example of suggestion and off-screen space. Fear emerges not only from what is shown, but also from shadows, sounds, waiting and what viewers imagine.

    Antonioni, M. (Director). (1960). *L’avventura* \[Film].\
    A character’s disappearance becomes a source of uncertainty and projection. The film demonstrates how absence can shape both a narrative and the relationships between its characters.

    Wise, R. (Director). (1963). *The Haunting* \[Film].\
    The threat is rarely shown directly. Sounds, doors, walls and unseen spaces transform the house into a disturbing presence.

    Buñuel, L. (Director). (1977). *That Obscure Object of Desire* \[Film].\
    The film explores desire as something elusive and unstable. It provides a useful example of how a narrative can conceal, displace or deliberately complicate its central object.

    Lanzmann, C. (Director). (1985). *Shoah* \[Film].\
    A major work for considering the ethics of what is not shown. The film draws on places, testimonies, silences and traces without using archival footage of the extermination itself.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Popular and contemporary films">
    Haneke, M. (Director). (2005). *Caché* \[Film].\
    Something acts from beyond the frame without being immediately identifiable. Viewers search for clues and experience the discomfort of never seeing or knowing everything.

    Martel, L. (Director). (2008). *The Headless Woman* \[Film].\
    The central event remains uncertain. Gestures, silences, details and absences invite viewers to reconstruct a situation that never becomes entirely clear.

    Farhadi, A. (Director). (2011). *A Separation* \[Film].\
    A partially unseen event generates contradictory accounts. What was not directly witnessed becomes the focus of a moral and narrative investigation.

    Chandor, J. C. (Director). (2013). *All Is Lost* \[Film].\
    With very little dialogue or explanation, the story develops through gestures, material traces and visible consequences. Viewers understand the situation through observation and deduction.

    Lowery, D. (Director). (2017). *A Ghost Story* \[Film].\
    Empty spaces, a vanished body and a house marked by traces explore how absence can become a continuing presence.

    Triet, J. (Director). (2023). *Anatomy of a Fall* \[Film].\
    The central event is never established with certainty. Sounds, testimonies, hypotheses and interpreted fragments are used to reconstruct several possible versions of what happened.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Bibliography">
    Bonitzer, P. (1982). *Le champ aveugle: Essais sur le cinéma*. Gallimard.\
    A particularly relevant introduction to off-screen space, absence and the elements that an image organises without representing directly.

    Burch, N. (1986). *Une praxis du cinéma*. Gallimard.\
    A foundational reference for studying on-screen and off-screen space, audiovisual form and the construction of the viewer’s perception.

    Aumont, J. (1989). *L’œil interminable: Cinéma et peinture*. Librairie Séguier.\
    A useful exploration of framing, perception, representation and the position assigned to viewers.

    Mondzain, M.-J. (2002). *L’image peut-elle tuer?* Bayard.\
    An essay addressing the power and limits of images, their effects and the responsibilities involved in showing or withholding.

    Didi-Huberman, G. (2004). *Images malgré tout*. Les Éditions de Minuit.\
    A more demanding reference examining what images can and cannot show, particularly when dealing with events that are difficult to represent.

    Niney, F. (2009). *Le documentaire et ses faux-semblants*. Klincksieck.\
    A useful work for discussing evidence, traces, clues and the relationships between the visible and the invisible in documentary forms.
  </Disclosure>

  <Disclosure title="Suggestions for trainers">
    * Select excerpts in which the central event is not immediately visible. Begin with a simple question: *What did you understand without seeing it directly?*
    * Then identify the elements that produced this understanding: a sound, a closed door, a displaced or missing object, an empty room, a change in light, a repetition or a material trace. Encourage learners to distinguish carefully between what the excerpt shows and what they infer.
    * You can also compare two ways of presenting the same event: showing it directly or suggesting it through off-screen space and clues. Discuss the different effects produced, such as intensity, ambiguity, restraint, suspense, mental projection or critical distance.
  </Disclosure>
</Callout>

## Conclusion

This module has shown that an audiovisual work can make an event understandable without representing it directly. Framing defines what remains unseen, while sounds, objects, traces and transformations of space guide the viewer’s attention and interpretation.

Learning to analyse off-screen space also means recognising the viewer’s active role. Meaning does not reside only within the visible image: it emerges from the relationship between what is shown, what is withheld and what each viewer reconstructs.
