This resource is based on the presentation by Federica Maietti and Andrea Sterpin, developed for the ARTEMIS learning activities on 3D documentation and digital cultural heritage. It builds on the concepts introduced in the session and uses examples from cultural heritage documentation to explain how three-dimensional data are acquired, processed, managed, and reused.

## **Introduction**

Cultural heritage is increasingly exposed to a wide range of natural and human-induced risks. Climate change, natural disasters, environmental degradation, and conflicts threaten monuments, archaeological sites, historic buildings, museum collections, and documentary heritage. Damage to these assets often results in an irreversible loss of cultural knowledge, making documentation an essential component of heritage protection. International initiatives have therefore recognized digital technologies as an important means of preserving knowledge about cultural heritage and supporting its long-term conservation.

Among these technologies, three-dimensional (3D) documentation has become one of the most significant developments in cultural heritage practice. Unlike conventional two-dimensional documentation, 3D approaches enable the recording of the geometry, appearance, and spatial relationships of heritage assets in a measurable and reusable digital form. These digital representations support not only the recording of the current condition of an object or site but also its analysis, conservation, interpretation, management, and communication. As digital technologies continue to evolve, 3D documentation also provides the foundation for interactive applications, including virtual and augmented reality, as well as for the emerging concept of Reactive Heritage Digital Twins.

This learning resource introduces the principles of 3D documentation for cultural heritage and explains how different survey methods, data management practices, and modelling approaches contribute to the creation of reliable digital representations. Rather than presenting individual technologies in isolation, it emphasizes that heritage documentation is fundamentally a methodological process. The objective is not simply to produce a visually convincing 3D model, but to create reliable, well-documented, and reusable knowledge that can support research, conservation, management, and public communication.

The resource first examines the motivations for heritage digitization before introducing the principal approaches to 3D modelling. It then discusses the importance of metadata, paradata, interoperability, and FAIR data principles, followed by an overview of reality-based documentation methods, including range-based, image-based, and topographic techniques. Finally, it presents recent developments such as AI-supported workflows, different categories of 3D models, and their role within Reactive Heritage Digital Twins.

## **Learning Outcomes**

After completing this module, learners will be able to:

* **Understand key challenges in heritage digitization** and recognize the importance of critical assessment in planning, producing, and interpreting 3D heritage data.
* **Identify the main principles, techniques, and instruments used in 3D survey and documentation** for cultural heritage.
* **Assess the strengths, limitations, and appropriate applications of different 3D acquisition methods**.
* **Explain the role of metadata, paradata, and data management** in making 3D documentation reliable, interoperable, and reusable, and connect these workflows to the broader concept of heritage digital twins.

## **Target Audience**

This learning resource is aimed at students, early-career researchers, and heritage professionals in the fields of cultural heritage, archaeology, architecture, conservation, and the digital humanities. It is suitable for learners seeking an introduction to contemporary 3D documentation methods and their role in heritage research, conservation, and management.

## **Intended Impact**

This module provides learners with a solid understanding of the principles and workflows of 3D documentation for cultural heritage. By introducing key acquisition methods, data management practices, and the concept of Reactive Heritage Digital Twins, it promotes critical and informed use of digital technologies and supports the creation of reliable, reusable, and interoperable heritage data.

## **Requirements**

No prior experience with 3D documentation or digital surveying is required. A basic understanding of cultural heritage and an interest in digital methods are recommended. The module is designed as an introductory resource and can be completed independently or as part of a university course or professional training programme.

## **Heritage Documentation and Digital Innovation**

The documentation of cultural heritage has always been a fundamental activity for research, conservation, and management. Accurate documentation records the current condition of monuments, archaeological sites, historic buildings, and artefacts, providing a basis for future investigations and interventions. Today, digital technologies have considerably expanded the possibilities for documenting cultural heritage by allowing the acquisition, management, and dissemination of large amounts of information.

The growing importance of digital documentation is closely linked to the increasing vulnerability of cultural heritage. Natural disasters, environmental change, and human activities can alter or destroy cultural assets within a very short period of time. International organisations and European research programmes therefore recognise digital documentation as an essential component of heritage preservation strategies. Rather than replacing the physical heritage itself, digital documentation preserves knowledge about heritage assets and provides a reliable record that can be consulted and reused in the future.

Over the past decade, several European initiatives have promoted the digitization of cultural heritage and have highlighted the importance of common standards, interoperability, and long-term accessibility of digital resources. These initiatives emphasise that digital documentation should support scientific research, conservation practice, education, and public dissemination while ensuring that digital data remain accessible and reusable over time.

Within this context, three-dimensional documentation has become one of the most significant technological developments in heritage documentation. Compared with conventional two-dimensional documentation, 3D technologies capture not only the visual appearance of an object or monument but also its geometry and spatial relationships. As a result, they provide a comprehensive representation that can support a wide range of applications throughout the documentation and conservation process.

## **Why 3D in Cultural Heritage?**

The use of three-dimensional technologies in cultural heritage serves several complementary purposes.

The first purpose is **documentation**. Three-dimensional acquisition techniques allow the detailed recording of buildings, monuments, archaeological sites, and artefacts by capturing their geometry together with visual and contextual information. The resulting datasets provide an accurate digital representation of the documented heritage asset and form the basis for subsequent analyses and applications.

A second purpose is **analysis**. Three-dimensional documentation supports scientific investigation by enabling accurate measurements and geometric analyses. It also provides valuable information for conservation planning and contributes to the understanding and interpretation of cultural heritage. Reliable three-dimensional data therefore become an important source of information for researchers and heritage professionals.

A further objective is **digital preservation**. High-quality digital documentation contributes to safeguarding cultural memory against degradation, natural disasters, and conflicts by preserving detailed records of heritage assets in digital form. Although digital documentation cannot replace the original heritage, it provides an enduring source of knowledge that may become particularly valuable if the physical asset is altered or lost.

Finally, three-dimensional documentation supports **communication**. Digital models facilitate the visualisation of cultural heritage for different audiences, including researchers, conservation professionals, cultural institutions, educators, and the general public. Through digital platforms and interactive visualisation, 3D models contribute to education, dissemination, and public engagement.

These different purposes demonstrate that 3D documentation is not limited to the production of visually realistic models. Instead, it represents a versatile tool that supports documentation, research, preservation, and communication within a common digital framework.

## **3D Modelling Approaches**

Different approaches can be adopted when creating three-dimensional models of cultural heritage. The choice of approach depends on the available information, the objectives of the documentation project, and the intended use of the resulting model.

**Source-based modelling** relies on historical and documentary sources. Drawings, photographs, maps, written documentation, and other archival materials provide the basis for reconstructing buildings, monuments, or archaeological remains. This approach is particularly relevant when documenting heritage that has been modified or no longer exists in its original form. Since the resulting models are derived from documentary evidence, their quality depends on the availability and reliability of the sources used.

**Reality-based modelling** is based on direct measurements acquired through survey technologies. Instead of reconstructing geometry from historical documentation, this approach captures the existing physical object using instruments such as laser scanners or digital cameras. The resulting models provide reliable geometric information because they are derived from measured data rather than interpretation. Reality-based modelling therefore plays a central role in documentation, conservation, monitoring, and research.

A third approach is **hybrid modelling**, which combines documentary sources with measured data. Historical documentation can complement reality-based surveys by providing information about missing elements, earlier construction phases, or features that are no longer preserved. By integrating different sources of information, hybrid modelling enables more comprehensive digital representations of cultural heritage.

Rather than representing competing methodologies, these three approaches should be understood as complementary strategies. Depending on the objectives of a documentation project, one approach may be more appropriate than another, while in many cases a combination of documentary evidence and direct measurements provides the most comprehensive representation of a heritage asset.
